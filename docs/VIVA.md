# FloodLens — Viva Voce Preparation

Below are **40+ viva questions** with student-friendly answers covering the
"why" and "how" of FloodLens. Practice explaining them in your own words.

---

### 1. Why did you choose flood prediction as your project?
Floods are frequent, destructive natural hazards. Multi-factor ML can help
stratify risk transparently, which is useful for community awareness and
planner decision support. It is also a domain where you can demonstrate
the full data-science pipeline — cleaning, EDA, feature engineering,
modelling, explainability, deployment — on meaningful, publicly available
data.

### 2. Why data science and not just a threshold-based system?
Single rainfall thresholds (e.g., ">100 mm → alert") ignore soil, land
cover, river conditions, elevation and history. ML can combine these
factors and produce a calibrated probabilistic risk instead of a rigid
rule.

### 3. What is FloodLens?
A Flask web application that classifies flood risk into Low / Moderate /
High / Critical using environmental and historical features; it provides
per-prediction explanations, an API, a dashboard and history.

### 4. Why did you choose Python and Flask?
Python is the standard for data science, and Flask is lightweight,
flexible, and integrates cleanly with joblib-saved scikit-learn models.
It also deploys easily to free hosts.

### 5. Why vanilla HTML/CSS/JS instead of React/Node?
The project is data-science focused. A simple stack is easier to
understand for a college project, lighter to deploy on free tiers, and
does not introduce unnecessary tooling.

### 6. Which dataset did you use?
The preferred dataset is "Flood Risk in India" from Kaggle (CC0 Public
Domain). If unavailable, a clearly labelled synthetic fallback with
matching schema is generated.

### 7. What is the license of the dataset?
CC0 Public Domain for the real dataset — free for any use. The synthetic
fallback is generated by this project under the project's MIT license but
must be labelled as synthetic.

### 8. Why did you choose CC0 data?
It allows unrestricted use, redistribution and deployment, which is
important for a public GitHub project and free hosting.

### 9. What is the target variable?
A four-level ordinal risk: Low, Moderate, High, Critical. It is derived
from a continuous risk score built from rainfall, discharge, water level,
humidity, elevation, soil, land cover, infrastructure and historical
floods; observed flood events are promoted to at least High.

### 10. Why classification instead of regression?
For early warning, stakeholders need actionable categories more than a
raw continuous score. Four levels map naturally to precaution tiers.

### 11. Why four classes, not two?
Binary (flood/no flood) loses gradations useful for preparedness. Four
classes support graduated responses from "routine awareness" to "act now".

### 12. How did you handle missing values?
Median imputation for numerics (robust to outliers) and mode imputation
for categorics, applied inside a scikit-learn pipeline so the same
statistics apply at inference time.

### 13. How did you handle outliers?
We apply physically plausible clipping (e.g., rainfall between 0 and 2000
mm) rather than statistical trimming, to avoid discarding extreme events
that are exactly what we care about.

### 14. What features did you engineer?
`Rainfall_72h_proxy` (√rainfall, antecedent wetness proxy),
`Rainfall_x_Level` (rainfall × water level), `Discharge_x_Humidity`,
`Elevation_inv` (1/(elev+10)). Each is documented.

### 15. Why did you choose these features?
They capture non-linear interactions and hydrologically intuitive
effects: wet catchments respond faster, higher river levels with high
discharge are dangerous, and low-elevation areas drain poorly.

### 16. What is data leakage and how did you prevent it?
Leakage happens when test-time information influences training. We
prevent it by wrapping preprocessing and the model in a single
scikit-learn Pipeline, so scaling/encoding is fit only on the training
fold.

### 17. Why train/validation/test splits?
Train is used to fit, validation to choose/tune the model, and test to
report a final unbiased estimate of performance.

### 18. Why stratified splits?
The classes are imbalanced; stratified sampling preserves class
proportions in each split for more reliable metrics.

### 19. Why not chronological splitting?
The chosen dataset has no reliable timestamp column for the rows we use,
so stratified random splitting is the defensible default. This is
documented as a limitation.

### 20. Which algorithms did you compare?
Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting,
and optionally XGBoost.

### 21. Why Logistic Regression?
On our data it gave the best macro-F1 and recall, is fast to train and
serve, and is highly interpretable — good for an educational project and
lightweight deployment.

### 22. Why Random Forest / Gradient Boosting?
They are strong tabular baselines: RF is robust to overfitting via
ensembling; GB sequentially corrects errors. We included them to justify
model choice rather than picking a single algorithm arbitrarily.

### 23. Why didn't you use deep learning?
Tabular flood data with mixed types and limited size is typically
handled very well by tree ensembles and linear models; deep learning
adds complexity without clear benefit for this scope.

### 24. What is overfitting?
When a model learns noise/patterns in training that do not generalize,
producing excellent train scores but poor test scores.

### 25. What is underfitting?
When a model is too simple to capture the underlying pattern (e.g., a
linear model on a strongly non-linear problem) leading to poor training
and test performance.

### 26. How do you detect overfitting?
Compare training vs validation performance; a large gap indicates
overfitting. Cross-validation provides a more stable estimate.

### 27. What is cross-validation?
Splitting the training data into k folds, training on k-1 and evaluating
on the remaining fold, repeated to produce a more robust performance
estimate and reduce lucky-split bias. We used 3-fold stratified CV in
tuning.

### 28. Why class_weight='balanced'?
To up-weight under-represented classes (important for risk systems where
missed critical events are costly).

### 29. Explain accuracy vs precision vs recall vs F1.
- **Accuracy:** fraction correct overall — misleading when classes are imbalanced.
- **Precision:** of all predicted positives, how many are real? Minimizes false alarms.
- **Recall:** of all real positives, how many did we catch? Minimizes misses.
- **F1:** harmonic mean of precision and recall.

### 30. Which metric is most important here?
Recall (and macro-F1) for higher risk classes, because missing a high-risk
event is more dangerous than triggering a false alarm. That is why we
selected the model with the best macro-F1 with recall tie-break.

### 31. What is the confusion matrix?
A table showing actual vs predicted classes, revealing which classes the
model confuses. It is displayed on the dashboard.

### 32. What is the macro vs weighted F1 difference?
Macro-F1 weights all classes equally, while weighted-F1 weights by
support. Macro is more appropriate when minority classes (e.g., Critical)
matter.

### 33. How did you tune hyperparameters?
With scikit-learn's `RandomizedSearchCV` over a small parameter grid
using 3-fold stratified CV, scoring by macro-F1. This balances
computation and result quality.

### 34. Why RandomizedSearchCV instead of GridSearchCV?
It is more compute-efficient; random search often finds good
hyperparameters faster than exhaustive grid search.

### 35. Explain your feature-importance/explanation method.
We use a transparent method: for numeric features, how many standard
deviations the input is from the training mean (with directional sign),
and for categorical features the difference between that category's mean
risk and the global mean. This avoids heavy dependencies while providing
useful, honest explanations.

### 36. Why didn't you use SHAP?
SHAP is powerful but adds non-trivial dependency weight and compute
cost. For a free deployment target we chose a lighter approach that is
still transparent. SHAP can be added later.

### 37. What's the difference between correlation and causation here?
Feature importance/explanation shows which variables most influenced the
model's prediction, not that those variables cause floods. We state this
explicitly in the UI.

### 38. How is the model persisted?
Using joblib: a single serialized Pipeline containing both preprocessing
and the tuned classifier, plus a JSON metadata file.

### 39. Why SQLite?
It's file-based, zero-administration, and perfect for prediction history
in a demo without introducing a server database. Model artifacts stay in
joblib, not in the DB.

### 40. How do you prevent the model from retraining every request?
The pipeline is loaded once via lazy singletons in `src/predict.py`.
The Flask process reuses it for every request.

### 41. Describe the API.
- `GET /api/health` returns a liveness JSON.
- `POST /api/predict` accepts JSON features and returns risk level,
  probabilities, explanations, version.
- `GET /api/metadata` returns model summary.
Errors produce HTTP 400 with a list of issues.

### 42. How did you validate inputs?
All fields are bounded (e.g., humidity 0–100), categoricals restricted to
allowed values, types coerced, and errors returned as a list so users
can correct them.

### 43. What security practices did you apply?
CSRF protection on forms (Flask-WTF), SECRET_KEY from environment, debug
off in production, no hardcoded secrets, input validation, safe error
pages without stack traces.

### 44. How do you deploy FloodLens for free?
Render (free tier): connect the GitHub repo, set build command
`pip install -r requirements.txt && python scripts/train_pipeline.py --synthetic`,
start command `gunicorn wsgi:application --bind 0.0.0.0:$PORT --workers 2 --timeout 120`.
Set `SECRET_KEY` and `FLASK_ENV=production`. A Procfile and render.yaml
are provided. Free tier has cold starts.

### 45. What are the limitations?
Geographic coverage (India), target derivation (4-class from risk score),
synthetic fallback when real data is absent, no time-series modelling,
no real-time sensor feeds, possible distribution shift, and not being an
official warning system.

### 46. Ethical issues?
The model must not be treated as an official warning. False negatives
can be dangerous. We prioritize recall for high-risk classes, include
visible disclaimers, and clearly label synthetic data.

### 47. What would you add next?
Real-time weather APIs, spatiotemporal models (LSTM/TFT), GIS/Leaflet
maps, IoT sensor integration, continuous monitoring and retraining,
multi-region training, alerting via email/SMS and a mobile app.

### 48. Walk me through the code structure.
- `app/` – Flask factory, routes, services (DB), templates, static assets.
- `src/` – data processing, feature engineering, training, prediction, visualization.
- `models/` – saved pipeline + metadata (generated by training).
- `data/` – raw/processed/synthetic CSVs.
- `tests/` – pytest suite.
- `scripts/` – convenience CLI (train, ensure_model).
- `docs/` – project report, viva, presentation.
- `run.py`, `wsgi.py`, `Procfile`, `render.yaml` – execution/deployment.

### 49. Why is a pipeline important?
It bundles preprocessing and model together, prevents leakage, ensures
train/serving parity, and makes deployment a single file to load.

### 50. What makes this a good college project?
It covers the whole ML lifecycle end-to-end on a meaningful problem,
uses a documented real (CC0) dataset with a transparent synthetic
fallback, compares multiple models with proper evaluation and tuning,
provides explanations, ships a tested web app and API, and is ready for
deployment — while honestly documenting limitations and ethics.
